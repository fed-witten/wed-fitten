Recap and aim for today

Last time we completed the proof of the following proposition:

Proposition 1. Let \(R\subset S\) be two rings and let \(\xi\in S\). Then the following are equivalent:

  1. \(\xi\) is integral over \(R\);
  2. The ring \(R[\xi]\) is an \(R\)-module of finite type;
  3. \(R[\xi]\) is contained in a ring \(T\), which is an \(R\)-module of finite type.

We assumed that \(R\) is Noetherian to give a quick proof of \((c)\Rightarrow (a)\). This assumption is not necessary, and when \(R\) is not Noetherian the result follows by a version of the Cayley-Hamilton theorem for modules that I do not plan to write up; see yesterday’s post for references. Coordinate rings of varieties and rings of integers of number fields are the rings we are most concerned with, and these are always Noetherian.

Our main goal for today is to show that given an extension of rings \(R\subset S\), the set of elements of \(S\) that are integral over \(R\) forms a ring. This will follow from a sequence of easy corollaries of Proposition 1.

Some corollaries of Proposition 1

Corollary 1. Let \(\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_n\in S\) be integral elements over \(R\). Then the ring \(R[\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_n]\) is an \(R\)-module of finite type.

Proof sketch. This is an easy induction argument.

The base case \(n=1\) is the implication \(a\Rightarrow b\) from Proposition 1.

If the proposition holds for each positive integer less than \(n>1,\) then we see that \[A[\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_n]=A[\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_{n-1}][\xi_n]\] is a finite type \(A[\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_{n-1}]\)-module by the base case, and in turn \(A[\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_{n-1}]\) is a finite type \(A\)-module by the induction hypothesis. Take a system of generators for each, then take all products of these generators; this forms a finite system of generators for \(A[\xi_1,\xi_2,...,\xi_n]\) as an \(A\)-module.

\(\square\)

Corollary 2. If \(\alpha,\beta\) are integral over \(R\), then so are \(\alpha+\beta\) and \(\alpha\cdot\beta.\)

Proof. By Corollary 1, \(R[\alpha,\beta]\) is a finite type \(R\)-module. Since \(R[\alpha+\beta],R[\alpha\cdot\beta]\subset R[\alpha,\beta]\), the implication \(c\Rightarrow a\) from Proposition 1 implies \(\alpha+\beta\) and \(\alpha\cdot\beta\) are integral over \(R\).

\(\square\)

In particular, we see that \(R[\alpha]\subset S\) is integral over \(R\) if and only if \(\alpha\) is integral over \(R\), and the elements of \(S\) that are integral over \(R\) form a ring.

Example 1. When \(R=\ZZ\) and \(S=\overline{\QQ}\), the elements of \(\overline{\QQ}\) integral over \(\ZZ\) are called the algebraic integers. When \(K/\QQ\) is a finite field extension, the set of elements of \(K\) integral over \(\ZZ\) is called the ring of integers of \(K\), typically denoted \(\cO_K\).

Corollary 3 If \(R\subset S\subset T\) are rings with \(T\) integral over \(S\) and \(S\) integral over \(R\), then \(T\) is integral over \(R\).
